Some mornings, my six-year-old sits down at the table and simply... doesn't start. He's not being difficult. He's frozen. And I know from hard experience that if I push into the lesson anyway, especially ELA, we're headed for tears. His or mine, possibly both.

So I stopped pushing. Now I open the game box instead.

That's the whole routine: when starting is the hardest part, we play a game first. Five to ten minutes of cards on the kitchen table, and by the time we're done, he's regulated, he's laughing, and his brain is already warmed up without realizing it ever started "school."

## The game isn't a delay. It's the on-ramp.

Think about it the way you'd think about exercise: you wouldn't ask a kid to sprint cold. So why ask him to start reading cold? The first ten minutes of our day aren't lost to play. They're the warm-up that makes the next hour possible.

Here's what surprised me most: the games we reach for are doing real academic work. Phonics, blending, sight words, you name it. Zeke is reading the entire time and calls it a game. I'm a math teacher and I'll be honest, ELA is not my strongest subject, so a game that does the phonics heavy lifting for me is worth its weight in gold.

## What's in our game box

I keep a small box of quick activities and games within arm's reach of the table. Nothing fancy. The rule is that everything in it has to be short (under ten minutes) and easy to start with zero setup, because the whole point is lowering the barrier, not adding one.

These are the two that get the most use:

**My Turn, My Turn** (our favorite ELA warm-up). This is the cow card game Zeke asks for by name. It's made for beginning readers: you practice consonant blends, short vowel sounds, and blending sounds into real words, all in five to ten minutes. The suspense of the draw keeps him hooked, and "one more round" is the most common sentence in our morning. **[Pop for Blends](https://amzn.to/4zx2eep)** (our blends game). This one comes in a little popcorn box and it's as fun as it sounds. Players draw popcorn pieces, build words with beginning blends and digraphs, and if you pull a POP kernel, all your pieces go back. The suspense is the whole point: Zeke will practice blends for ten minutes straight because he desperately wants to avoid the POP. This routine has been such a win that I'm deliberately growing the box. Right now it's ELA-heavy, so I just ordered three math games to balance it out. Once we've played them all in, I'll share our five favorites and what each one is actually teaching. Consider this post part one.

## How to make it work in your house

1. **Keep the box visible and reachable.** If the games are buried in a closet, they won't happen on a hard morning. Ours lives right next to the table.
2. **Set a timer, not a round count.** "One more round" can turn into forty minutes. Ten minutes of play, then we transition. The timer makes the ending blameless.
3. **Let them choose sometimes.** Zeke picks the cow game almost every time, and that's fine. Choosing is part of the buy-in.
4. **Play with them.** Five minutes of your attention at the start buys you an hour of independent focus later. It's the best trade you'll make all day.
5. **Don't grade the game.** The minute it feels like a test, it's not a warm-up anymore. Laugh, be silly, lose on purpose sometimes.

## The reframe that changed our mornings

I used to think of the stuck mornings as a discipline problem: he won't start, so I need to make him start. Now I think of them as a warm-up problem: he can't start yet, so I need to help him get there.

The game box didn't just change our mornings. It changed how I think about the whole day. Learning doesn't always have to begin with the lesson. Sometimes it begins with cards on the kitchen table, a six-year-old giggling over a cow, and a mom who finally stopped fighting the morning.
